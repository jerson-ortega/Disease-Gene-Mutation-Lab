# Investigating How DNA Sequence Changes Affect Protein Products and Human Phenotypes
# Background

Many human diseases and biological traits are associated with changes in DNA sequence. A mutation may involve one nucleotide or many nucleotides. It may change an amino acid, introduce a premature stop codon, shift the reading frame, remove amino acids, add amino acids, or affect gene function in another way.

In this laboratory, you will investigate one human disease or medically relevant phenotype and gene. You will study the normal function of the gene, obtain its normal coding sequence (CDS), translate it to obtain the predicted normal protein sequence, manually reproduce a documented mutation, translate the mutant sequence, and compare the normal and mutant proteins.

Your main goal is to explain the following relationship: DNA mutation -> change in coding sequence -> change in protein -> change in molecular or cellular function -> disease or phenotype.

# Important Scientific Distinction

Translation of a DNA sequence predicts the protein sequence encoded by that CDS. It does not demonstrate that the protein is actually expressed in a human cell. Protein expression, abundance, localization, folding, and activity require additional experimental evidence. Use the term "predicted protein sequence" when discussing the output of sequence translation.

# Part II. Research the Disease

**A. Disease** 
Familial hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (FHC) is a genetic cardiovascular disorder primarily characterized by unexplained left ventricular hypertrophy (thickening of the heart muscle) (Argirò et al., 2026). This structural abnormality can lead to heart failure, arrhythmias, and is a leading cause of sudden cardiac death in young athletes. The cells and tissues predominantly affected are the cardiomyocytes (cardiac muscle cells) within the myocardium. The genetic basis involves mutations in genes encoding sarcomere proteins, with MYH7 being one of the most frequently implicated (Brögger et al., 2026). The inheritance pattern is typically autosomal dominant, meaning a single mutated copy of the gene is sufficient to cause the disease phenotype.


**B. Gene and Normal Protein**
The official gene symbol is MYH7, located on human chromosome 14 at band 14q11.2. The gene encodes the cardiac beta-myosin heavy chain. The normal biological function of this protein is to act as a molecular motor within the thick filaments of the sarcomere. By hydrolyzing ATP and mechanically interacting with actin thin filaments, the beta-myosin heavy chain generates the force required for normal cardiac muscle contraction.

Part IX. Compare WT and Mutant Proteins
Sequence comparison between the wild-type and documented mutant protein products reveals that the two sequences first differ at amino acid position 403, where Arginine (R) is replaced by Glutamine (Q) in the translated mutant. Translation shows that only this single amino acid is affected. No amino acids were deleted or inserted, nor was any premature stop codon produced within the reading frame. Because the single-base substitution does not shift the triplet sequence alignment, the overall reading frame remains intact and the protein length stays unchanged at 1935 amino acids. Classification of this variant identifies it as a pathogenic missense mutation.

# Part X. Explain the Molecular Consequence

At the genomic level, the mutation is a single-nucleotide substitution that changes the 1208th base pair of the MYH7 coding sequence from Guanine to Adenine. This alters codon 403 (from CGG to CAG) without altering the downstream reading frame or causing a frameshift.

Translation produces a full-length 1935-amino-acid sequence with a single point substitution (p.Arg403Gln). Because this specific amino acid substitution occurs precisely within the highly conserved motor domain of the beta-myosin heavy chain (where myosin interacts with actin), it alters the biophysical properties and enzymatic activity of the protein. The mutated myosin incorporates into the cardiac sarcomere but physically impairs actin-myosin cross-bridge cycling. To compensate for this mechanical inefficiency and maintain cardiac output, the cardiac muscle cells undergo pathological hypertrophy, manifesting clinically as the thickened ventricular walls characteristic of Familial hypertrophic cardiomyopathy.

# Prediction before translation artificial mutant
In the second controlled sequence experiment, an artificial single-nucleotide deletion was introduced at nucleotide position 1208 of the MYH7 coding sequence. This deletion shifts the boundary of every subsequent codon relative to the reading frame. In-silico translation using the Transeq tool confirmed that this frameshift alters the downstream amino acid sequence and rapidly introduces a premature stop codon shortly after position 403. Consequently, translation terminates prematurely, yielding a severely shortened, non-functional peptide compared to the 1935-amino-acid wild-type protein. This mutation results in a complete loss of the structural tail domains of the myosin heavy chain, producing a non-functional truncated protein that would likely be degraded in vivo via nonsense-mediated mRNA decay.

# Part XIII. Interpretational questions

32. Why does the exact location of a mutation matter?
- The exact location dictates whether critical functional sites such as initiation codons, active catalytic residues, or signal peptides are disrupted. A mutation within the essential motor domain of MYH7 significantly alters biomechanical activity, whereas a change in a non-critical region might have minimal impact on overall function.

33. Why can deleting three nucleotides produce a different result from deleting one or two nucleotides?
- Deleting three nucleotides removes exactly one codon, resulting in an in-frame deletion of a single amino acid while preserving the original reading frame for all downstream sequences. In contrast, deleting one or two nucleotides (as seen in the artificial c.1208delG mutation) alters the triplet grouping of every subsequent codon, shifting the entire downstream reading frame and typically leading to extensive missense sequences followed by a premature stop codon.

34. Does every mutation change the amino-acid sequence? Explain.
- Not every mutation changes the amino acid sequence because the genetic code is degenerate, meaning multiple codons can specify the same amino acid. Single-nucleotide substitutions often yield synonymous codons that translate into the exact same protein sequence.

35. Does every amino-acid substitution destroy protein function? Explain.
- No, not every amino acid substitution destroys protein function. Conservative substitutions that swap an amino acid for one with similar size, charge, and chemical properties often preserve protein folding and function. However, non-conservative substitutions (like the R403Q variant) occurring at catalytic or structural sites are far more likely to disrupt activity.

36. Why can a frameshift affect many amino acids even if only one nucleotide was deleted?
- A frameshift affects numerous amino acids because protein translation relies on reading mRNA in continuous, non-overlapping triplets. Deleting even a single nucleotide shifts the boundary of every subsequent codon relative to the ribosome, altering the identity of all downstream amino acids until translation encounters a new, out-of-frame stop codon.

37. Why might a premature stop codon produce a nonfunctional protein?
- A premature stop codon results in an incomplete, nonfunctional protein because it eliminates downstream structural and functional domains required for proper folding, ligand binding, or localization. Additionally, transcripts bearing premature termination codons are frequently targeted and degraded by cellular quality-control pathways, such as nonsense-mediated mRNA decay (NMD).

38. Could a mutation affect protein function without greatly changing protein length?
- Yes, it can severely impair protein function without altering its overall length. As demonstrated by the c.1208G>A documented mutation, a single amino acid missense substitution alters key active-site mechanics without adding or subtracting amino acids from the 1935-amino-acid polypeptide chain.

39. Could a mutation cause disease without changing the protein sequence? Give a possible molecular mechanism.
- Mutation can cause disease without altering the coding sequence by targeting gene regulatory regions. Mutations in promoters, enhancers, or splice sites can disrupt transcription factor binding or cause aberrant pre-mRNA splicing, eliminating or severely reducing protein expression despite an unchanged coding region.

40. What evidence from your analysis supports the proposed molecular mechanism of your disease?
The computational analysis of the MYH7 c.1208G>A mutation shows that altering the nucleotide from Guanine to Adenine maintains the downstream open reading frame but causes an Arginine to Glutamine substitution at position 403. This computational evidence supports the mechanism of a biomechanically defective but structurally intact protein, which necessitates compensatory hypertrophy by the heart muscle to maintain adequate systemic circulation.

41. Which conclusions are supported directly by your computational results, and which require evidence from published experimental studies?
Computational results directly support conclusions regarding sequence alignment, codon changes, reading frame preservation, and predicted amino acid substitutions (like p.Arg403Gln). Proving that this variant alters cross-bridge cycling dynamics in the sarcomere and leads to clinical cardiac hypertrophy requires evidence from published experimental and clinical studies.
